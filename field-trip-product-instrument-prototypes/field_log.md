### Crux Sieve prototype: field notes from the first run

_2026-08-13T20:40:22.926Z_

Crux Sieve prototype: field notes from the first run
This test exposed a useful instrument with a weak entry gate. The sieve did its best work after the case, horizon, reach, and aim were explicit. Before that point, it made it too easy to produce a polished reading from the wrong frame and an incomplete corpus.

What happened
I first treated prior Expedition material as enough to freeze the case. That was wrong. The inherited material centered Kyle’s acquisition and career choices, while the intended case was Neon’s new BaaS: how Electric’s sync and realtime work might fit, and how the combined team could build the highest-market-share BaaS within two years. Kyle stopped the run and required an interview.

The interview repaired the frame one answer at a time:

Aim: highest BaaS market share, in the ordinary sense.
Horizon: two years.
Reach: normal product, engineering, and marketing control.
Fixed constraints: none identified; the effort is new and planning begins in fall 2026.
The corpus then grew to 18 sources. It covered Neon’s emerging backend, the Electric acquisition story, PGlite and sync, Supabase, and Firebase. The sieve separated service completion, Electric integration, shared state semantics, distribution, analytics depth, and more abstract model-choice studies. It returned one candidate crux at the product–distribution seam and preserved an important-but-unaddressable control plus an easy-but-low-value control.

That separation was useful. The reading did not collapse every important problem into “the crux,” and it marked the result as a candidate rather than a decision. It also named the evidence gaps: no audited cross-vendor share measure, no observed backend-selection rates, no retention data, no demand test for local/cloud sync, and no integration-feasibility evidence.

Friction and likely instrument changes
1. Add a hard corpus-and-direction check before the sieve starts
The card says the instrument requires enough evidence to reject some candidates, but the procedure does not force the operator to demonstrate that readiness. It moves from freezing the case straight into listing live problems.

A new Step 0 should require a short readiness record:

the user has confirmed the case, aim, horizon, reach, and intended decision;
the corpus covers the main candidate categories and obvious alternatives;
enough evidence exists to reject at least one plausible candidate;
material omissions and internal-only evidence are named;
the operator states what new evidence could overturn the result;
the user confirms that this is the direction they meant to examine.
If this check fails, the instrument should stop. It should return the missing evidence or framing questions rather than continue with a thin sieve.

This run shows why. I prepared and started the run against the inherited career frame before Kyle corrected it. The same run later continued under a different case. A stronger lifecycle rule would treat a material boundary change after preparation as a stopped run followed by a newly selected run, preserving cleaner provenance.

2. Add a coverage control for omitted competitors and candidate classes
The source set was opportunistic rather than demonstrably sufficient. It included Supabase and Firebase but omitted Convex. Kyle caught the omission only after the reading was complete.

The instrument needs a pre-readout check such as: “Which live competitor, substitute, architectural approach, or user theory would make this candidate look less pivotal?” This is not a demand for exhaustive research. It is a guard against calling the sieve complete when a material part of the candidate space has not entered the corpus.

3. Guard against elegant compression
The card already warns that the sieve may let a candidate action define the diagnosis. That happened in a subtler form: the reading compressed product architecture and distribution into one clean story. It emphasized becoming the backend that agents select by default, while under-specifying Kyle’s product idea—a fully branchable, declarative reactive system, defined with few tokens and suited to many agents building in parallel.

The instrument should require a final decomposition check:

Is this one obstacle, or two coupled obstacles?
Which part is product, distribution, organization, or market condition?
Does joining them reveal leverage, or merely make the candidate sound complete?
What user-supplied idea was lost when the candidate was compressed?
The present uncertainty remains real: the candidate may be one product–distribution crux, or it may be two mutually reinforcing cruxes that must move together.

4. Check temporal and causal wording
My later wording said to build the system and “then” make it the default BaaS. Kyle corrected this: product development and the work of becoming the default will happen in parallel.

Before returning a causal trace, the sieve should label relations as sequential, parallel, reciprocal, enabling, or merely correlated. This would prevent prose from inventing a roadmap that the evidence and user did not supply.

5. Require a usable success measure—or mark its absence earlier
“Highest market share” was enough to orient the interview, but not enough to test pivotality with confidence. The run found no common audited denominator and no shared measure of created, activated, retained, or paid projects.

The instrument should distinguish a directional aim from an operational success measure during intake. It may still run without the latter, but the readout should then say at the top that pivotality is being judged against a coarse objective whose measurement remains unresolved.

Recording-layer friction
Two problems belonged to the Field Log workflow rather than the Crux Sieve itself.

First, completed readings are append-only. Kyle’s corrections appeared as later feedback and notes, while the app remained pinned to the original readout. The corrected hypothesis was therefore hard to see until it received its own diary entry and link. The reader needs a clearer way to surface superseding notes beside the earlier run without rewriting history.

Second, while recording one correction, I created a temporary JSON payload with a direct file-editing tool before passing it to the CLI. The canonical Field Log was still changed only by the CLI, but the extra step violated the intended CLI-only workflow. That was operator error, not an instrument defect. Later writes used append --json directly.

Remaining uncertainty
The run did not establish whether the candidate crux survives a corpus that includes Convex, internal Neon research, channel partners, customer demand, agent task success, and retained usage. It also did not establish whether a dominant-BaaS objective can be measured consistently across vendors.

The strongest conclusion from the test is therefore about fit, not strategy: the Crux Sieve looks useful as a second-stage instrument once a focused case and sufficient candidate corpus exist. It is not yet safe as a discovery instrument. Its next revision should add a user-confirmed readiness gate, an omission check, a decomposition check, and explicit temporal labeling before it returns candidate cruxes.

### Instrument test diary: Strategic Power Audit

_2026-08-13T20:43:03.277Z_

Instrument test diary: Strategic Power Audit
2026-08-13 · reflections after two runs on Neon BaaS

How it went
The instrument's core discipline worked: it kept asking for both an economic Benefit and a durable Barrier, and it refused to turn technical difficulty, product quality, adoption, or a current lead into Power by themselves. The capable-rival counterfactual was especially useful. The question was not merely whether Supabase could copy Neon, but whether Supabase—or another rival—could neutralize the customer outcome without copying Neon. The negative control also worked: ordinary preview deployments correctly returned advantage without Power.

The first run nevertheless exposed a serious readiness problem. I had enough material to produce a fluent ledger, but not enough clarity to know that I was auditing the right claim. I froze the case too early around three separate inputs—Neon's cost architecture, Databricks primitives, and Electric sync—and treated Supabase as the rival field. That frame missed the user's actual compound thesis: build a fully branchable, declarative reactive system for agent swarms while making it the default BaaS. It also missed Convex as the semantic rival and Firebase as the distribution rival.

The first result was not false within its narrow frame, but it answered a weaker question. The instrument's own rules eventually exposed the missing economic and barrier evidence, yet only after the audit had already run. A readiness gate should have caught the thin corpus and unclear direction first.

The interview repaired the frame. Run 2 became much more useful once branching was made central, value and capture were separated, distribution was made concurrent with construction, and the rival set covered three distinct response paths. The second ledger found a specific architectural asymmetry rather than a generic bundle of strengths. It still returned indeterminate Power, but for a sharper reason: the evidence does not yet show whether rivals must bear Neon-like architectural cost or can satisfy the agent job through substitutes.

What worked well
Benefit and Barrier stayed separate
This was the strongest part of the instrument. Neon can have lower branch-provisioning cost without possessing Power. Electric can bring rare expertise without a measured customer or cash-flow Benefit. A default-choice ambition can matter strategically without yet forming a barrier. The separate statuses prevented these claims from laundering one another.

The rival was allowed to respond intelligently
The audit improved when it gave rivals their actual strengths. Supabase attacks through Postgres familiarity, product breadth, distribution, and price. Convex attacks through reactive semantics, agent state, and durable workflows. Firebase attacks through Google's selection funnel and cloud primitives. This made exact feature matching much less important than job-level neutralization.

The negative control calibrated the word “branching”
Every platform can provide some form of preview, clone, or isolated deployment. Treating ordinary previews as the control prevented the instrument from awarding Power merely because a product uses the word branching. The candidate mechanism had to narrow to cheap, coherent forks of meaningful live backend state, reactive coordination, and safe effect handling.

“Indeterminate” was a useful result
The instrument did not force a binary verdict. It could identify an architectural barrier candidate while marking the customer Benefit, cash-flow capture, rival cost, and duration as unmeasured. That was more honest than either endorsing the thesis or dismissing it because public financial evidence was absent.

Friction and operator uncertainty
1. No mandatory corpus-readiness gate
The card lists required inputs, but it does not require an explicit decision that those inputs are present in adequate form. I should have had to classify the corpus before preparing the ledger:

Ready: enough evidence exists to test Benefit, Barrier, and at least one capable-rival response.
Provisional: the direction is clear, but the corpus supports only a scenario reading and evidence-gap map.
Not runnable: the business, market, claim, rival, or minimum evidence cannot yet be fixed.
This case should initially have been marked provisional. The corpus contained vendor claims, technical descriptions, and adjacent evidence, but little customer behavior, cash-flow data, or rival-relative cost evidence. That status should have appeared before any Power classification.

2. The strategic direction emerged too late
The instrument asked me to freeze the business, market, period, and claim, but it did not force a check that the claim matched the user's actual objective. “Evaluate Neon's BaaS” admitted several audits: current company Power, the value of Electric sync, the feasibility of a product thesis, or a future route to dominance. I selected one too soon.

A preflight should ask one explicit direction question: Are we auditing current Power, a proposed future Power, or whether a product thesis could become Power? Those require different evidence and different treatment of absence.

3. Benefit began too close to cash flow
The cash-flow discipline was valuable, but the procedure made it easy to skip over use value. In run 1, I moved quickly from technical inputs to cost and revenue while under-describing why whole-system branching might matter to an agent swarm. Run 2 needed two linked chains:

product condition → user or agent capability → observed behavior;
behavior → selection, willingness to pay, cost, investment, retention, and cash flow.
The instrument should require both. Otherwise it risks declaring Benefit unmeasured without first making the proposed value legible enough to test.

4. Compound systems do not fit cleanly into “do not aggregate”
The warning against combining weak claims is correct, but the actual thesis was conjunctive: Neon architecture, Electric's reactive model, Databricks primitives, and concurrent distribution may create an outcome none produces alone. Run 1 decomposed the system and lost the interaction. Run 2 restored the compound claim, but the card offers no explicit rule for doing this safely.

The procedure should permit a compound mechanism only when the interaction itself is stated and tested. The operator should show why A + B + C changes Benefit or Barrier, rather than adding three weak scores.

5. One capable rival was not enough
“At least one capable rival” was too weak for this market. A single rival encouraged me to choose the nearest product analogue and miss other neutralization routes. A rival-coverage check should consider:

the direct architectural rival;
the same-job, different-architecture substitute;
the distribution or platform owner that can make the feature secondary.
Not every audit needs three named companies, but the corpus should cover the materially different response paths.

6. Key terms needed decomposition before classification
“Branching” initially bundled deployment previews, database clones, snapshots, whole-system forks, merging, rollback, and agent isolation. “Reactive” and “durable workflows” also concealed different mechanisms. The audit improved only after those terms were separated.

The instrument needs a short ambiguity check for any term carrying the claimed Benefit or Barrier. If rivals implement different meanings of the same word, the ledger should define the minimum customer outcome before comparing products.

7. Barrier evidence was hard to calibrate
Public sources can show that Neon built a distinctive storage architecture and that extending its semantics across services is hard. They cannot readily show rival engineering cost, opportunity cost, time to neutralization, or whether another architecture is adequate. I was uncertain how much architectural observation should count toward a supported Barrier.

The safest result was indeterminate. The instrument would benefit from a calibration rule: technical difficulty supports a barrier candidate; supported Barrier requires evidence about a capable rival's inability, unacceptable economics, structural conflict, or late arrival.

8. Renewal under continuous attack remained underspecified
The user's “only the paranoid survive” standard made durability dynamic. Run 2 proposed that distribution, integrations, telemetry, and operating learning might renew the lead. The instrument did not say how to test a renewal mechanism. A current barrier and a repeatable improvement engine are different claims.

A dynamic audit should require evidence about learning rate, release rate, accumulated process, or response cycles—not merely state that the company can keep innovating.

Suggested revision to the procedure
Add a mandatory Step 0: Corpus and direction check before freezing the case.

The operator should confirm:

whether the audit concerns current Power, proposed future Power, or Power potential;
the exact business, market, period, claim, and minimum customer job;
at least one source beyond aspiration for the proposed Benefit;
at least one observed mechanism for the Barrier;
capable-rival evidence covering the main substitution routes;
a negative control;
contrary evidence and known evidence gaps.
The check should return Ready, Provisional, or Not runnable. A provisional run may produce a hypothesis ledger and evidence requirements, but it should not produce a firm Power classification.

Then revise the main sequence:

Freeze the case and define ambiguous load-bearing terms.
Trace use value before economic capture.
State and quantify the Benefit.
State the Barrier and distinguish current exclusion from renewal.
Build a rival-coverage grid around copying, substitution, distribution, and price.
Test the negative control.
Classify Benefit, Barrier, and the whole claim with corpus readiness visible beside the result.
Bottom line
The instrument was useful, but too eager to run. Its Benefit × Barrier core, capable-rival test, and negative control all earned their place. The main failure was upstream: it could turn an insufficient or misdirected corpus into a polished ledger before admitting that the corpus was insufficient or the direction was wrong.

This test suggests the Strategic Power Audit should be a second-stage assay after a short readiness check—or should stop itself and return a bounded research gap when readiness fails. The first run showed how a weak frame can produce a technically disciplined answer to the wrong strategic claim. The second run showed that once the compound claim, value chain, rival coverage, and evidence limits were explicit, the same instrument produced a much sharper result.
