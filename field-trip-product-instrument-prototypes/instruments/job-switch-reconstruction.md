# Job-switch reconstruction (`job-switch-reconstruction`)

- **Phenomenon / range / input:** The progress a person sought in one concrete circumstance and the forces that shaped an actual hire, switch, workaround, abandonment, or decision not to act. The unit is a choice episode, not a demographic segment, product category, or timeless need.
- **Why use it:** Surveys and direct “why did you buy?” questions often return preferences, product attributes, or polished reasons. Reconstructing the event can expose the trigger, alternatives, anxieties, habits, and social or emotional stakes that made the choice possible.
- **Procedure:**
  0. Make a readiness record before naming a job. Confirm the actor, actual episode, approximate date, prior way of doing the work, chosen or rejected solution, evidence source, and whether the account can recover a sequence rather than only a reason. Do not supply a candidate job in the intake question. Ask the user to confirm the episode. Return **Ready**, **Documentary provisional**, or **Not runnable**. A Documentary provisional run may return a partial story and missing questions but no supported job statement. If there is no actual episode or source-preserved behavior, stop as Not runnable.
  1. Freeze one actor and one episode. Keep later episodes separate until each reconstruction is complete. If the actor, decision, or time window changes materially, stop and ask whether the user wants a new run.
  2. Elicit or extract the story in the actor's order before imposing categories: “Take me back to when this first became a live problem.” Preserve exact phrases and mark interviewer prompts, source claims, and later inferences separately.
  3. Reconstruct the timeline: prior routine, first thought, passive looking, active search, alternatives considered, decisive events, commitment, first use, and later outcome. Mark gaps and contradictions rather than smoothing them.
  4. Trace the four forces with episode evidence: **push** away from the old situation, **pull** toward the new one, **habit** holding the old way in place, and **anxiety** resisting the new choice. A stated opinion without a scene or behavior remains weak evidence.
  5. Describe the sought progress in the circumstance. Separate functional, social, and emotional dimensions only when the account supports them. Do not infer a social or emotional job to make the result feel complete.
  6. Identify every competing hire: direct products, another category, manual work, an internal tool, help from another person, a workaround, delay, or doing nothing. Compare what each option helped the actor accomplish in that circumstance.
  7. Test the account against action. Ask what changed in behavior, what tradeoff the actor accepted, what evidence would show the stated reason was retrospective rationalization, and what would have happened if the old way remained available.
  8. Form one or more **candidate job statements** only from the reconstructed record: “When [circumstance], I want to [progress], so I can [outcome].” Link every clause to the episode and keep rival formulations when the evidence does not distinguish them.
  9. When several episodes are available, complete Steps 1–8 separately before comparing them. Look for repeated circumstances, forces, and competing hires; do not average them into a persona or erase a negative case.
  10. Freeze the negative control: a persona, broad need, feature request, or direct answer to “why did you buy?” with no recoverable sequence. Confirm that it returns `unsupported job claim` rather than a polished job statement.
  11. Return readiness, the corrected episode timeline, force diagram, progress dimensions, competing hires, candidate job statements with trace links, contradictions, and missing questions. Do not turn the reading into a segment, feature roadmap, positioning line, or product decision.
- **Readout / control:** A source-preserved episode record with timeline, push, pull, habit, anxiety, functional/social/emotional progress, competing hires, tradeoffs, behavior after the choice, and candidate job statements. Include the abstract-preference control, interviewer influence, contradictions, and what remains unrecoverable.
- **Common artifacts:** Leading with a product hypothesis; interviewing about general behavior; accepting attributes as jobs; treating a feature request as progress; relying on a single polished reason; inventing emotional depth; excluding workarounds and nonconsumption; merging several actors too early; and turning a candidate job into a recommendation.
- **Escalate / stop:** Stop when no actual episode can be fixed, the source cannot recover behavior or sequence, or the episode changes after preparation. Return Documentary provisional when the record supports only part of the story. A later live interview, observation, or product test requires separate authority; this card does not choose what to build.
- **What it requires:** Careful interviewing or transcript work, access to one real episode, verbatim preservation, and enough time to revisit concrete moments. Multiple episodes need separate records before comparison.
- **Execution placement:** **Orchestrator.** The person or source holder must be able to correct the sequence, terms, and missing alternatives as the story forms. A fresh transcriber may prepare a source-preserved timeline when authorized, but must not name the job before the orchestrator or user corrects it.
- **Donor basis:** Clayton Christensen, Taddy Hall, Karen Dillon, and David Duncan's Jobs to Be Done account of progress in a circumstance, competing hires, nonconsumption, and the functional, social, and emotional dimensions of choice.
