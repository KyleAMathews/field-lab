# Crux sieve (`crux-sieve`)

- **Phenomenon / range / input:** A focused case containing several consequential problems or opportunities whose importance, central obstruction, and addressability differ. Use it only after direction and candidate coverage are explicit. It is a second-stage sieve, not a discovery instrument or task-list generator.
- **Why use it:** Ordinary prioritization often rewards urgency, size, confidence, or ease. This operation asks a narrower question: which evidenced obstacle is pivotal enough, and addressable enough, that concentrated effort could change the situation?
- **Procedure:**
  0. Make a readiness record before preparing the sieve. Confirm the case, aim, horizon, reach, intended decision, and whether the aim has an operational success measure. Show that the corpus covers the main candidate categories and obvious alternatives, can reject at least one plausible candidate, names material omissions and internal-only evidence, and states what new evidence could overturn the result. Ask the user to confirm the direction. If any material condition fails, stop and return only the missing evidence or framing questions.
  1. Freeze the confirmed boundary and preserve the user's wording. If the case, aim, horizon, reach, or intended decision changes materially after preparation, stop. Ask the user whether to select a new run under the corrected boundary; do not continue the old run across cases.
  2. List the live problems and opportunities from supplied evidence. Keep symptoms, causes, constraints, candidate actions, and product, distribution, organization, and market conditions separate.
  3. Check coverage: ask which live competitor, substitute, architectural approach, or user theory could make the leading candidate look less pivotal. Name material omissions; do not claim exhaustive coverage.
  4. For each item, state what makes it consequential and what evidence supports that importance. Do not turn importance into an invented score.
  5. Expose the central obstacle, tension, or paradox that makes each important item difficult. Mark unsupported causal accounts as hypotheses.
  6. Test addressability: identify the capability, authority, resource, coordination, or leverage that could act on the obstacle within the horizon. Record what remains outside reach.
  7. Test pivotality: state what else would become easier, unnecessary, or newly possible if the obstacle moved. Distinguish a pivotal link from a merely large problem.
  8. Freeze two controls when the case supports them: one important but presently unaddressable item, and one easy but low-value item. State when either control is unavailable.
  9. Challenge the leading candidate before return. Ask whether it joins two coupled obstacles; label each part as product, distribution, organization, or market; state whether the join reveals leverage or merely sounds complete; and name any user-supplied idea lost in compression.
  10. Label each claimed relation as sequential, parallel, reciprocal, enabling, or merely correlated. Do not turn a causal trace into an invented roadmap.
  11. Return zero or more **candidate cruxes**. For each, give the importance case, obstacle, addressability case, leverage, expected downstream change, relation labels, contrary evidence, omitted candidate classes, and excluded secondary issues. If the aim lacks an operational success measure, state at the top that pivotality was judged against a coarse unresolved objective. The user decides whether to adopt one.
- **Readout / control:** Start with the readiness record and any success-measure warning. Then give a candidate-crux table, coverage ledger, decomposition check, and labeled relations from challenge to obstacle, leverage, and expected downstream change. Controls are the important-but-unaddressable item, the easy-but-low-value item, and the strongest omitted candidate class. A null result is valid.
- **Common artifacts:** Choosing the easiest item; treating urgency as pivotality; hiding political constraints; converting goals into obstacles; making addressability a confidence score; letting the candidate action define the diagnosis; omitting a live rival; compressing coupled obstacles into one elegant story; inventing a sequence from parallel work; hiding a coarse success measure; treating the result as a decision.
- **Escalate / stop:** Stop when direction, coverage, or evidence readiness fails; when the boundary changes materially; or when importance, obstacle, or addressability cannot be distinguished. Return the missing framing or evidence instead of a thin candidate. Escalate to a substrate map when events and explanations are mixed, a stake map when importance is undefined, or a real-world check when one reversible action could test leverage. Do not run those instruments without selection.
- **What it requires:** A focused case, explicit horizon and reach, user confirmation, success-measure status, a candidate corpus broad enough to expose an omission, and evidence able to reject at least one candidate. Research is optional and requires separate authority.
- **Execution placement:** **Orchestrator.** The user's live account of importance, authority, and feasible reach is part of the specimen. A fresh subagent may challenge one addressability claim only if separately authorized; it does not select the crux. If the user's stakes or constraints are missing, stop with the unresolved comparisons.
- **Donor basis:** Richard Rumelt, *The Crux* and the strategy kernel. The transfer keeps the importance × addressability test and the central-obstacle focus, but returns candidates rather than choosing a strategy.
