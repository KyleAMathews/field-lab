# Organizational capability fit (`organizational-capability-fit`)

- **Phenomenon / range / input:** The fit between one initiative's required work and a defined organization's resources, repeated processes, and operative priorities. The same capabilities that support established work can disable work with different economics, cadence, customers, or coordination needs.
- **Why use it:** Capability is often reduced to talent or budget. An organization may have the people and technology for an initiative yet reject it through budgeting, sales, development, or margin rules—or lack a resource while its processes and priorities fit perfectly well.
- **Procedure:**
  0. Make a readiness record before diagnosing fit. Confirm the initiative, organizational unit, assessment date, horizon, required outcome, critical work, economic model, evidence of actual recurring processes, evidence of resource-allocation decisions, known gaps, and a resource-only control. Ask the user to confirm the direction. Return **Ready**, **Provisional**, or **Not runnable**. A Provisional run may return a capability hypothesis and evidence needs but no firm process or priority conflict. If the initiative, unit, or required work cannot be fixed, stop as Not runnable.
  1. Freeze the initiative–organization pair. Do not move between company, division, team, acquired firm, or partner levels. If the initiative, unit, horizon, or required outcome changes materially after preparation, stop and ask whether the user wants a new run.
  2. Translate the initiative into required work: decisions, skills, assets, coordination, cadence, quality threshold, customer type, sales motion, revenue timing, cost structure, and margin or investment profile. Keep proposed requirements labeled as hypotheses.
  3. Audit **resources** that can be hired, bought, transferred, or lost: people, skills, technology, product designs, data, cash, brands, channels, relationships, and physical assets. Record availability, control, quality, transferability, and the evidence for each material claim.
  4. Audit **processes** through repeated behavior, not manuals: product development, budgeting, prioritization, sales, hiring, procurement, compliance, integration, support, and decision handoffs. State which work each process was built to perform and where the initiative demands a different pattern.
  5. Audit **priorities** through actual allocation choices. Treat values as operative rules—not ethics—including acceptable margins, transaction size, market size, growth rate, payback period, channel, risk, customer class, and profit-formula constraints. Distinguish public statements from decisions under pressure.
  6. Test interactions. Ask whether the resources can work through the existing processes, whether those processes can deliver the required economics and cadence, and whether priority rules will keep allocating attention after the initiative competes with the core business.
  7. Examine historical analogues and near misses. Use prior initiatives, budget decisions, staffing changes, launches, integrations, or abandoned work to test the ledger. Failure alone does not locate the mismatch; preserve rival explanations.
  8. When acquisition or partnering is in scope, locate the capability. If it resides mainly in resources, integration may preserve it; if it resides in processes and priorities, assimilation may destroy it. Record this as a location finding, not an integration prescription.
  9. Freeze the negative control: an initiative with a bounded missing resource but compatible processes and priorities. Confirm that supplying the resource resolves the ledger without inventing a deep organizational conflict.
  10. Return readiness and separate statuses for resources, processes, and priorities: `fit supported`, `resource gap`, `process conflict`, `priority conflict`, `compound conflict`, or `indeterminate`. Show evidence, interaction effects, historical controls, and what would falsify each status. Do not recommend a heavyweight team, autonomous unit, acquisition structure, or reorganization unless the user separately asks.
- **Readout / control:** An initiative requirements table, resource ledger, process trace, priority and profit-formula evidence, interaction matrix, historical analogues, capability-location note when relevant, and separate bounded statuses. The resource-only case controls against turning every gap into organizational disability.
- **Common artifacts:** Treating values as ethics; equating capability with talent; reading org charts as processes; using stated strategy as allocation evidence; inferring conflict from one failure; assuming processes cannot learn; treating all mismatches as culture; recommending a spinout by reflex; and losing the initiative boundary.
- **Escalate / stop:** Stop when the initiative, organizational unit, required work, or allocation evidence cannot be fixed, when readiness is Not runnable, or when the boundary changes after preparation. Return Provisional when only stated processes or priorities are available. The card does not choose an organization design or assign blame.
- **What it requires:** Evidence of actual work and resource allocation, initiative-specific requirements, historical comparisons, and careful separation of available assets from habitual coordination and priority rules. Fresh research requires separate authority.
- **Execution placement:** **Orchestrator.** Live correction is needed to distinguish the formal organization from the unit that will actually execute and fund the initiative. A fresh reviewer may challenge self-reported process or priority evidence when authorized, but the orchestrator returns the bounded ledger and preserves the remaining correlation.
- **Donor basis:** Clayton Christensen and Michael Overdorf's resources–processes–values theory, plus the later business-model account of value proposition, resources, processes, and profit formula. The transfer preserves the distinction between what an organization possesses, how it repeatedly works, and what it will fund.
