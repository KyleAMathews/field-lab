# Taboo Parallax (`taboo-parallax`)

Use cross-society differences in sayability to expose assumptions and questions that one public sphere keeps out of view. The instrument adapts the spatial comparison in Paul Graham's [“What You Can't Say”](https://paulgraham.com/say.html), but adds a strict truth control: a costly statement may reveal a taboo without being true.

## Instrument contract

- **Phenomenon sought:** Claims, questions, distinctions, or premises that are ordinary in one social setting but euphemized, sanctioned, or excluded in another.
- **Why use it:** Reasoning inside one public setting makes its taboos feel like fact, taste, or simple decency. A controlled comparison makes differences in speech pressure, enforcement, and suppressed questions visible.
- **Operating range:** Social, political, organizational, moral, economic, family, professional, or technological topics where public norms shape which hypotheses can be stated. Do not use it to manufacture provocation, characterize an entire nationality, or settle a factual claim by popularity elsewhere.
- **Input:** A topic; the user's live question; the relevant home setting; the speech arena and audience; the present or historical time slice; and the decision the comparison should inform.
- **Perturbation:** Selecting countries, translating terms, and asking directly about taboos can create the very contrast being sought. Model priors and English-language sources can turn cultures into stock characters.
- **Readout:** A source-grounded sayability matrix, asymmetric blind-spot candidates, enforcement mechanisms, within-society countervoices, truth tests, source limits, and unresolved residue.
- **What it requires:** Three or four independent research tracks plus comparison. For a full run, offer to start a Field Log to keep the sources and comparisons together. A dialectic workflow may use the matrix as framing, donor, third-pole, or ground-condition material.

## Execution placement

- **Scout — orchestrator:** After the user selects it, the orchestrator runs the small hypothesis scan because it needs the live focus interview and must return the first map for immediate user correction. It may see the conversation and up to three orienting sources. No agent separation is claimed. If the context is already captured by a national stereotype, offer a fresh framing-sensitivity control or state the risk. The orchestrator returns the scout map without explaining what it proves.
- **Full run — hybrid:** The orchestrator freezes the comparison set and common matrix. Separate parallel research subagents each see one bounded public sphere, the topic, arena, time slice, and source contract—but not sibling findings, the hoped-for heresy, or a preferred national contrast. After all tracks return, the orchestrator builds the symmetric diff, venue gradient, truth gate, and user-facing matrix.
- **Placement rationale:** Isolated tracks prevent one country's story from scripting the others; the orchestrator retains the cross-case and user continuity needed for comparison.
- **Fallback:** If separate research contexts are unavailable, run a sequential source-grounded comparison labeled **correlated parallax scan**. Do not claim blind-track convergence or the full instrument.
- **Return path:** Research tracks return sourced local findings and gaps to the orchestrator; only the orchestrator builds the controlled comparison. It returns the matrix, source limits, and hypotheses without turning them into conclusions.

## State boundary and scout-first rule

A Walk may offer and scope a **taboo-parallax scout**. Run it only after the user selects it. A request for proof or a source-grounded comparison selects the full run rather than the scout. The scout returns a small provisional map, then leaves any choice about proof to the user.

After selection and before any source lookup or scout content, lead with the action and limit. For example: **“Let’s compare where this claim can be said plainly, softened, or not voiced in these settings. I’ll use a Taboo parallax scout to give us a small hypothesis map—not proof.”** Do this even when the user requested the scout by name, but vary the words to fit the case. It may use model priors and no more than three orienting sources total, but it may return only candidate speech locations, contrasts, and research questions. It may not claim what a society “won't say,” create a log, or launch country tracks.

The scout should test whether the real contrast is:

- between countries;
- among public arenas inside one country;
- between abstract agreement and a concrete utterance or action inside a power relation.

Return two or three concise candidate asymmetries, their provenance, and what evidence would be needed to establish each. Keep it a first map rather than a source dossier. Seek user correction before offering a full run.

A full run compares three or four source-grounded public spheres through a frozen matrix. Offer to start a Field Log so the sources and comparisons stay together. Explain the separate research tracks, amount of source work, and stereotype risk plainly, then get assent before creating files.

## Procedure

### 1. Focus the speech setting

Clarify the topic, the user's aim, and what result would matter. Ask the meaning of “won't say” and the social field as separate questions; do not pack them together. If the user says “both,” do not treat that as permission for maximum scope—choose a first exposure or run a scout across the ambiguity.

Replace “society” with a bounded arena where possible: national press, a profession, a religious community, a workplace class, a family setting, an academic field, or another named public.

Specify what “can't say” means in this run:

- awkward but ordinary;
- requires euphemism;
- contested but publishable;
- carries social or career cost;
- carries legal or physical sanction.

Absence from search results is not evidence of unsayability.

### 2. Freeze a diverse comparison set

Choose three or four societies or public spheres before collecting candidate taboos. Include the user's home setting when known. Include at least one close comparator that could defeat an exceptionalism story and at least one distant contrast that creates parallax. Justify the set using differences relevant to the topic—such as political system, religious settlement, family structure, market/state relation, media regime, or professional norms—not vague geographic variety.

Record the selection rule. Do not swap cases after seeing which comparison produces the most dramatic result.

### 3. Run blind local source tracks

Use a separate research track for each setting. Give each track the topic, arena, and time slice, but not the other tracks' findings or the hoped-for heresy.

Each track must report:

1. claims stated plainly in mainstream local discourse;
2. claims expressed only through euphemism or indirection;
3. labels used instead of a truth-directed rebuttal;
4. observed sanctions and who can impose them;
5. local dissenters and counterexamples;
6. original local terms where translation matters;
7. source strength and gaps.

Prefer current local-language evidence when feasible. Use at least one mainstream or institutional source and one countervoice per setting. Separate law, institutional policy, opinion polling, published controversy, and model inference.

Conflict records show that a cost occurred, not how common it is. Diversify publishers and source types; flag when searchable evidence clusters around one outlet, litigation, scandal, or failed institution.

### 4. Map the venue and power gradient

Test the same proposition at several speech locations where relevant:

- anonymous survey or private belief;
- mainstream publication or policy language;
- peer-to-peer discussion;
- subordinate to supervisor, worker to employer, dependent to gatekeeper;
- a refusal or action that makes the proposition govern the relationship.

A society may say a general belief loudly while punishing the concrete sentence that changes who bears a cost. When speech cost rises near control over pay, status, renewal, reputation, or access, report that power relation as a candidate ground condition rather than a national silence.

### 5. Build the symmetric diff

For each candidate claim, compare both directions:

- ordinary there, costly here;
- ordinary here, costly there;
- euphemized in both for different reasons;
- absent because it is irrelevant, false, or expressed through another category.

Name the audience and sanction. Do not write “Country X believes…” when the evidence covers one arena or constituency.

### 6. Apply the truth gate

Keep three layers separate:

1. **Speech-cost finding:** evidence that a statement is easier or harder to voice in a setting.
2. **Blind-spot hypothesis:** what the asymmetry suggests the home frame may not inspect clearly.
3. **Truth test:** evidence that would support, weaken, or falsify the underlying claim.

Sayability elsewhere does not validate a claim. Anger, labels, or sanctions may reflect threatened power, but they may also reflect prior harm, legal duties, etiquette, bad evidence, or a plainly false premise.

### 7. Return for correction

Show the matrix and measured asymmetries without turning them into conclusions. Ask the user which setting, translation, sanction, or candidate feels misread. Do not run another probe until the user selects it.

## Controls

- **Within-society control:** Find a countervoice and an internal division for every country or public sphere.
- **Symmetry control:** Ask what the home setting can say that each comparison setting suppresses, not only the reverse.
- **Selection control:** Freeze the sample and its diversity rationale before collecting findings; include a close comparator and a distant contrast.
- **Venue-gradient control:** Test whether the proposition is publicly sayable but costly when addressed to a person who controls resources or status.
- **Power control:** Check whether concentrated control explains the speech cost better than national culture.
- **Translation control:** Preserve local terms and note where English categories do not map cleanly.
- **Absence control:** Require positive evidence of speech cost; silence alone proves nothing.
- **Truth control:** Pair every blind-spot hypothesis with an empirical or argumentative test.
- **Temporal control:** Do not mix eras; mark rapid norm changes and source dates.
- **Source control:** Distinguish direct local evidence from outside commentary and model inference; mark conflict-record and publisher concentration bias.

## Common artifacts

- National stereotypes replace bounded public spheres.
- Countries are chosen because they promise an edgy contrast.
- A taboo is treated as evidence that the tabooed claim is true.
- Legal prohibition, moral fashion, etiquette, and factual rebuttal collapse into one category.
- English-language prominence is mistaken for local salience.
- Elite discourse stands in for the whole population.
- A public statement is mistaken for freedom to make the same statement govern an employment or dependency relation.
- Conflict records are used to infer prevalence rather than possibility and mechanism.
- Translation manufactures agreement or opposition.
- The probe rewards shock value and ignores ordinary but consequential silences.

## Escalation and stop

Offer a ground-condition probe when enforcement or institutional power may account for the asymmetry; a stake map when different groups may bear its costs; belief stress when the user wants a tabooed position inhabited rather than merely listed; or historical comparison when the present contrast may be transient. Do not auto-run the next instrument.

Stop when the instrument has produced a bounded speech-cost finding and a testable question. Do not keep searching for more forbidden claims merely to make the output provocative.
