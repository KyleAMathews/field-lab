# Behavior chain (`behavior-chain`)

- **Phenomenon sought:** The route through which one bounded human action became possible or useful in its setting: prior vulnerabilities, a prompting event, reported links, the target action, and its immediate and later consequences. For a completed or abandoned switch, the optional switch branch also seeks the forces that promoted and restrained change as they appeared across the timeline.
- **Why use it:** A person may remember only the action and a broad explanation such as “I froze” or “I always do this.” A purchase account may collapse months of pressure and hesitation into “I liked it, so I bought it.” A chain makes the smaller links available for correction and shows where several functions or switch forces remain possible.
- **Operating range:** Use for one action, lapse, avoidance, escalation, repeated move, or completed or abandoned switch reconstructed from the actor's first-person account or direct records. Work from one recent or well-recorded episode. Do not infer another person's conduct from analytics, second-hand anecdotes, or a prospect's hypothetical intent; assign fault; deliver crisis care; or press into material the person does not want to revisit. Do not use it to shift responsibility onto someone living under coercion, abuse, or another person's target conduct.
- **Input:** One behaviorally stated target action or switch, a rough episode boundary, and the actor's first-person account or direct records. Ask for missing material one question at a time. Let the person set the depth and stop at any point.
- **What changes:** The operation slows a global account into a sequence and asks about thoughts, feelings, body sensations, actions, and environmental events. This can cue detail and impose order, so treat every link as reported or inferred rather than discovered.
- **Procedure:**
  1. **Set one target.** Restate one observable action in the user's words, bound the episode, and ask for correction. Do not bundle several problems into one chain.
  2. **Mark vulnerabilities.** Record conditions already present before the episode that the user thinks may have mattered. Keep context separate from cause.
  3. **Locate the prompting event.** Identify the event after which the chain began. If the boundary is unclear, preserve the alternatives.
  4. **Trace links in order.** Ask one question at a time for the next remembered environmental event, action, thought, feeling, or body sensation. Preserve the user's wording and mark gaps rather than filling them.
  5. **Record the target action.** State what the person did or did not do without a trait label or judgment.
  6. **Record consequences.** Separate immediate from later effects and effects the user wanted, expected, disliked, or noticed only afterward.
  7. **Offer competing functions.** State two or more plausible accounts of what the action changed, gained, delayed, escaped, communicated, or protected. Tie each to reported links and name evidence that would weaken it. Do not infer a hidden motive.
  8. **Run the optional switch branch.** Use it only for one actual completed or abandoned switch. Place reported events on a loose timeline—first thought, passive looking, active looking, decision or abandonment, and actual use when present—without manufacturing missing stages. Then code each event with zero or more supported forces: **push** from the current situation, **pull** toward the new option or imagined progress, **anxiety** reported as fear or uncertainty about the new option or switch, and **habit** reported as familiarity or inertia preserving the present way. Let one event carry several forces or none. Do not relabel an objective obstacle such as a deadline, policy, price, or migration task as anxiety without reported uncertainty; do not infer habit merely because the old behavior continued. Keep the person's language, mark each code as reported or inferred, and record evidence that would change it.
  9. **Separate decision from outcome.** For a switch, distinguish the progress imagined before the decision from the consequences reported after use. Do not treat purchase, abandonment, or later satisfaction as proof of the force balance that caused it.
  10. **Return for correction.** Show the chain, gaps, competing functions, and any switch-force coding. Ask one correction question and stop. Do not move into solutions, product recommendations, or cross-interview synthesis unless the user asks for that separate task.
- **Result:** Return the target boundary; vulnerabilities; prompting event; ordered, typed links; target action; immediate and later consequences; competing functional hypotheses; support and gaps; and the person's corrections. For the switch branch, also return the event timeline, per-event push/pull/anxiety/habit coding, imagined progress, actual consequences when known, alternate codes, and missing evidence. Label inner experience as testimony and functional or force assignments as hypotheses unless the person stated them directly.
- **Control:** Keep the original account beside the chain. Mark each element as first-person report, direct record, or inference. Preserve alternate prompt boundaries and competing functions. For switches, code events before summarizing forces, permit missing, mixed, and uncoded events, keep objective conditions separate from subjective forces, and compare imagined progress with later reported use. The person, not the model, confirms whether the sequence fits.
- **Common distortions:** Recall becomes false precision; “vulnerability” becomes defect; consequence becomes intention; one plausible function becomes the reason; the four forces become a complete motive taxonomy or literal equation; every obstacle becomes anxiety and every continuation becomes habit; a purchase is treated as proof that push plus pull caused the switch; the chain becomes a long interrogation; context becomes blame; or the model moves from understanding to advice without permission.
- **Escalate / stop:** Stop when the user asks to stop, the target will not stay singular, recall no longer supports order, or continuing would require clinical care. A solution-focused operation may follow only by user request after the chain is corrected.
- **What it requires:** Several short exchanges or a first-person source account collected by [`open-page`](open-page.md). Ask one question at a time and reflect personal material before probing. A Field Log helps only when the correction trail or source records need to persist.
- **Execution placement:** **Orchestrator.** Live contact and correction create the access. A fresh agent cannot recover unreported experience and should not infer it from prose. If live contact is unavailable but a direct first-person record exists, return a source-limited draft chain with gaps and unconfirmed force codes, not a completed reading.
- **Distinctness:** Unlike [`substrate-map`](substrate-map.md), this instrument includes user-reported internal links, prior vulnerabilities, consequences, and competing functions. Unlike a solution analysis, it stops before proposing repairs.
- **Provenance:** The core is a nonclinical Field Lab port of DBT behavior chain analysis. It retains the donor practice's target behavior, vulnerability factors, prompting event, links, consequences, and solution boundary while narrowing the use to user-led inquiry. See the [Oxford Handbook of Dialectical Behaviour Therapy](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/38169/chapter/333011509) and [Behavioral Tech's chain and solution analysis training](https://behavioraltech.org/event/10-10-2022-strengthening-your-practice-of-dbt/). The optional switch branch adapts Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek's official accounts of the [switch timeline](https://jobstobedone.org/the-timeline/), [Four Forces of Progress](https://jobstobedone.org/the-four-forces/), and [Switch Interview](https://jobstobedone.org/switch-interview/). The card treats the four forces as revisable coding of one account, not a validated causal equation. The Field Lab card and its LLM use have not been validated as therapy or customer-research measurement.
