# Situated evidence dossier (`situated-discourse`)

- **Phenomenon sought:** The source-visible field in which participants understood, made, contested, and revised a bounded history: the episodes, positions, local terms, conventions, actions, constraints, interactions, contradictions, and later reassessments that could support more than one honest account.
- **Why use it:** Timelines preserve events and Research Surveys preserve a landscape of positions, but both can dissolve the interaction through which an act became a promise, refusal, joke, warning, category claim, concession, or misfire. A polished narrative solves the opposite problem too early: it selects one arc and hides the material that other stories would need. This operation builds a reusable, inspectable evidence field before editorial choice.
- **Operating range:** Use for one consequential episode or a bounded formation preserved in messages, mailing lists, forums, interviews, transcripts, documents, issue threads, recordings, releases, or other attributable digital traces. The record may include later recollections and user-supplied field material. It needs participant-produced evidence and enough context to test more than one reading of important acts. Do not use for an unbounded topic, anonymous aggregates, or a record containing only outcomes and exterior observations. It is not automated ethnography and does not recover unexpressed thought, feeling, tacit practice, private discussion, or embodied context.
- **Input:** Choose one entry path. **Corpus-first:** freeze the period or episode boundary, intended downstream uses, source cutoff, and existing attributed trace corpus. **Collection-first:** obtain explicit authority for source research, then freeze the period or episode boundary, intended downstream uses, source classes and venues, query or retrieval plan, exclusions, cutoff, budget, and stopping rule before collection. Both paths must produce a frozen trace corpus before interpretation. Give every trace a stable ID and record its speaker or author, role, time or supported range, audience, medium, contemporaneous or retrospective status, source kind, interest, content pointer, and access limit. Keep later accounts outside the contemporaneous corpus until the retrospective overlay.
- **What changes:** The operation turns a source collection into a selective dossier of situated social evidence. It restores sequence, position, local meaning, contest, and source absence, but it also chooses a boundary, divides the record into episodes, foregrounds some voices, and assigns analytic labels no participant authored. It does not select a thesis, narrative arc, opening scene, or ending.

## Procedure

1. **Freeze the field and entry path.** State what opens and closes the period or episode, what kinds of later questions the dossier may support, and what use is excluded. For a corpus-first run, record which sources are included, excluded, missing, deleted, private, or inaccessible and freeze the corpus. For a collection-first run, record the exact user authority and freeze the source classes and venues, query or retrieval plan, exclusions, cutoff, budget, and stopping rule before searching. Do not shape either boundary around a preferred outcome or story.
2. **Complete and freeze the corpus.** A corpus-first run performs no new collection. In a collection-first run, collect and register traces only within the frozen plan; preserve queries, retrieval coverage, exclusions, failures, and the stopping condition. Then freeze the resulting trace corpus before interpreting it. If the source landscape or participant set remains too open to bound, stop and offer `research-survey`; do not improvise an expanding search inside the dossier.
3. **Declare the evidence mode and register trace types.** Use **polyphonic** when attributable traces preserve at least two materially different participant positions; **single-position** when only one participant horizon survives; and **exterior-only** when no participant construction survives. Stop an exterior-only run with a thin evidence boundary. Do not present single-position material as a shared account. For each trace, record provenance and distinguish direct utterance or action, participant recollection, participant interpretation, document claim, observed artifact change, and analyst inference. Mark pseudonymous, interested, translated, excerpted, or decontextualized material. Do not add sources after the corpus freeze without a new user-selected collection pass.
4. **Map coverage before interpretation.** Show the distribution of traces across time, participants, roles, media, source kinds, and contemporaneous versus retrospective accounts. Mark dense and sparse regions, missing sides of exchanges, and whose records are likely to survive or disappear. Do not let source volume stand in for social importance.
5. **Build the thin spine.** Record only sourced utterances, actions, artifact changes, responses, and consequences in supported order. Preserve disputed time, missing intervals, asynchronous audiences, edits, deletions, and unknown receipt. Do not insert motive, inevitability, or causal linkage.
6. **Index episodes without choosing a plot.** Divide the field into bounded episodes around traceable interactions, decisions, breakdowns, repairs, category disputes, or changes in practice. For each episode, record its boundary, participants, material setting, trace IDs, observable change, open questions, and connections to other episodes. Keep low-drama and failed episodes when they bear on the field. Do not rank episodes, pitch stories, or select an opening scene.
7. **Reconstruct participant horizons.** At each consequential moment, state what each represented participant could access from the record, the terms they used, their stated aims and stakes, what alternatives or constraints they named, and what the corpus does not establish they knew. Attribute feelings only when reported or directly expressed; label any reading of tone or affect as inference.
8. **Recover local codes and success conditions.** Identify participant terms, conventions, roles, precedents, and material conditions that made an act count as one thing rather than another. For each load-bearing act, record the candidate action, intended or attributed audience, message, code, success or misfire condition, and source support. Preserve different meanings by position and time instead of normalizing them.
9. **Trace interaction and change.** Record how utterances, acts, tools, and institutional moves answered, ignored, constrained, reframed, or altered one another. Show which later move made an earlier act look different. Keep silence and absence as missing response unless the record establishes refusal, assent, ignorance, or exclusion. Mark causality as a participant claim or analyst inference rather than converting sequence into mechanism.
10. **Preserve rival meanings, contradictions, and negative cases.** Give source-compatible readings for consequentially ambiguous acts when the corpus supports them. Mark each link `[fit: solid|plausible|reach]`, name disconfirming traces, and state what missing evidence would separate the readings. Record events that resist the dominant chronology, category, success account, or apparent direction. Do not create a weak rival merely to decorate a favored one.
11. **Overlay later reassessment.** Add retrospective participant accounts, later institutional narratives, and later outcomes only after the contemporaneous records are frozen. Show what each adds, revises, contradicts, forgets, or smooths. Do not let outcome knowledge silently rewrite earlier participant horizons.
12. **Assemble the editorial handoff.** Return a source-linked dossier containing the boundary, trace and coverage registers, thin spine, episode index, participant horizons, local-code register, interaction and change chains, rival meanings, contradictions, negative cases, retrospective overlay, source gaps, and ethical exclusions. Give each item a stable pointer so a later workflow can select and combine evidence without changing this record. Do not add candidate essays, theses, titles, pitches, outlines, a preferred causal spine, or a public narrative.
13. **Run the controls.**
    - **Thin control:** Compare every claim about meaning, stakes, codes, or horizons with the thin spine and its trace support.
    - **Position ablation:** Remove one load-bearing participant position and record which parts of the dossier disappear, weaken, or become one-sided. If nothing changes, that position was probably flattened.
    - **Retrospective isolation:** Reconstruct one important episode without later accounts, then state exactly what the overlay changed.
    - **Anti-arc control:** Group the same evidence by participant or local code instead of time. Mark material the episode sequence made peripheral or falsely coherent.
    - **Exterior negative case:** State what would remain from artifact changes and public outcomes alone. It must not retain unsupported interior or local-meaning claims.
14. **Audit the making.** List boundary and episode-selection rules, source-selection effects, analyst-supplied labels and bridges, compressed or omitted material, inaccessible positions, ethical redactions, and unresolved readings. End with what remains unmeasured, not with a total account.

- **Result:** A reusable, source-traced evidence dossier rather than a story: boundary and evidence mode; trace and coverage registers; thin spine; episode index; participant-horizon records; local codes and success conditions; interaction and change chains; rival meanings, contradictions, and negative cases; retrospective overlay; controls; construction audit; and unmeasured remainder. Store it as an instrument readout in the source Field Log. Later editorial work treats that log as read-only and cites stable trace or episode IDs. The dossier may support several conflicting stories; it does not decide among them.
- **Control:** The thin spine is the non-interpretive baseline. Position ablation tests whether plurality changes the record; retrospective isolation tests outcome leakage; the anti-arc control tests whether chronological grouping smuggled in a plot; the exterior negative case tests invented interiority. Source links and explicit analyst labels expose how the dossier was made. No density of detail compensates for missing participant access.
- **Common distortions:** Collection follows the story the researcher already wants; a launch or conflict crowds out ordinary practice and failed moves; founders become the authoritative voice; public speech is treated as private belief; vivid quotations substitute for context; one participant becomes “the community”; retrospective success rewrites uncertainty; silence becomes assent; asynchronous posts become a live conversation; local codes become a glossary detached from action; chronology becomes causality; or the dossier quietly contains a pitch and outline.
- **Escalate / stop:** Stop when the boundary cannot be defended, no participant-produced source survives, attribution is too weak to support participant horizons, or controls leave only exterior facts. Escalate to Research Survey when the source landscape or participant set is still unknown, to Substrate Map when chronology remains disputed, or to Formation Section when unit lineage matters more than participant meaning. Those operations require user selection unless already queued. Narrative construction belongs to a separately selected editorial workflow.
- **What it requires:** High-effort source extraction and close reading over a bounded corpus. A single executor can build a small settled dossier, but a fresh context is required when a prior survey, strategy claim, or preferred history could pre-shape the evidence field. Use a Field Log so trace IDs, revisions, corrections, and the downstream handoff persist.
- **Execution placement:** **Hybrid.** The orchestrator freezes the boundary, intended-use limits, entry path, trace schema, and either the existing corpus or the authorized collection plan without supplying a hoped-for interpretation. Any collection completes and freezes before dossier construction. A fresh subagent receives the card, frozen brief, and frozen source corpus—but not prior thematic summaries, product recommendations, a preferred causal story, or an outline—and returns the dossier and controls. The orchestrator verifies collection and source pointers, checks the construction audit, and records the bounded reading without choosing a narrative. If fresh context is unavailable, label the result a **correlated situated dossier** and do not claim the position-ablation control is independent. Same-model and shared-search correlation remain even with a fresh context.
- **Distinctness:** Unlike [`research-survey`](research-survey.md), this instrument reconstructs situated episodes and participant meanings rather than mapping a broad evidence landscape. Unlike [`substrate-map`](substrate-map.md), it seeks participant horizons and local codes rather than only an observable event sequence. Unlike [`formation-section`](formation-section.md), it preserves socially interpreted episodes rather than primarily tracing accumulated units and lineage. Unlike an editorial workflow, it does not choose a candidate, outline, or story.
- **Provenance:** The central distinction adapts Gilbert Ryle's thin/thick description through Clifford Geertz's account of ethnography as interpretation of social discourse, participant constructions, local structures of signification, inscription, microscopic grounding, appraisal, and intrinsic incompleteness. The trace and coverage registers, episode index, position ablation, retrospective isolation, anti-arc control, evidence-mode gate, editorial handoff, and LLM execution placement are Field Lab constructions for source-bounded digital records; they are not a canonical anthropological method and do not transfer the access created by field participation. See Geertz's [“Thick Description: Toward an Interpretive Theory of Culture”](https://hci.stanford.edu/courses/cs376/private/readings/geertz_thick_description.pdf).
