# General Writing Guide

Use this guide for every Essay draft. It sets a shared floor for clear,
source-faithful prose. It does not define a personal voice. A selected external
writer-voice profile may add constraints through
[writer-voice-profiles.md](writer-voice-profiles.md).

## Governing order

When instructions conflict, apply them in this order:

1. the user's current instruction;
2. the explicitly selected external writer-voice profile;
3. the frozen audience and publication constraints;
4. this general guide; and
5. model defaults.

Never let style alter evidence, confidence, attribution, uncertainty, or the
approved design without recording the change.

## Write for a reader

- Write for the reader named in the editorial brief. Assume only the knowledge
  the brief supports.
- State the main point early enough that the reader can orient to what follows.
- Put known context before new distinctions. Introduce a term before asking it
  to carry an argument.
- Prefer concrete actors and actions to abstract noun chains. Name who did,
  observed, claimed, or inferred what.
- Give each paragraph one main job. Make the relation between paragraphs
  explicit when sequence, contrast, cause, or qualification matters.
- Vary sentence length for pace and emphasis, not as decoration.

## Preserve the material

- Keep observation, testimony, source claim, inference, analogy, and judgment
  distinct.
- Preserve conflicts and awkward particulars when they matter. Do not smooth
  them into a cleaner theory.
- Do not turn sequence into cause, analogy into evidence, or a plausible bridge
  into a sourced mechanism.
- Use examples for a named job. Label generated examples as illustrations.
- Keep uncertainty visible at the point where it qualifies a claim.
- Reward close reading with mechanisms, sequences, evidence, particulars, and
  cross-section connections. Density is not the number of claims per paragraph.
- Break a passage when it asks the reader to track too many relations without
  help. Cut it when it only restates the overstory.

## Prefer plain, exact prose

- Use the shortest familiar word that keeps the exact meaning.
- Prefer active verbs when the actor is known and relevant.
- Cut throat-clearing, padding, false emphasis, teacherly recap, and repeated
  conclusions.
- Replace stale or mixed metaphors. Keep a metaphor only when it clarifies a
  relation that literal prose would hide.
- Avoid stock transitions and generic model phrasing. A transition should name
  the real relation between the passages it joins.
- Use technical language when it is the clearest exact language. Explain it
  once when the reader may not know it.
- Do not overstate a point merely to make the prose feel decisive.

## Bounded cleanup

Run at most three cleanup passes after preserving a pre-edit snapshot:

1. **Structure and orientation:** buried subjects, weak verbs, unclear paragraph
   jobs, missing transitions, and passages that make the reader hold too much.
2. **Compression and rhythm:** padding, repetition, stale phrasing, monotonous
   syntax, and needless restatement.
3. **Fidelity:** factual errors, unsupported claims, missing citations, shifted
   confidence, and prose that violates the selected voice profile.

Read difficult passages aloud. Use sentence-length counts, repeated-phrase
searches, and similar mechanical checks only as warnings. They are not quality
scores or universal quotas. Record every edit beyond mechanics for the
Semantic Drift Assay.

## Final questions

- Can the target reader state what changed between the opening and the end?
- Does each section do work the approved design assigned to it?
- Are actors, claims, sources, and uncertainty easy to locate?
- Does close reading provide more than a longer version of the summary?
- Did any cleanup make the prose smoother by making the account less true?
